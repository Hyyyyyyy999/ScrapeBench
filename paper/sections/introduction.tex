\section{Introduction}

The web has become the default training corpus for identity-hungry generative models, and faces are its most sensitive commodity. A handful of public portraits is now sufficient to synthesize a person in any context: DreamBooth-style fine-tuning can bind a rare token to an identity with four to eight images~\cite{ruiz2022dreambooth}, while zero-shot adapters such as InstantID and IP-Adapter-FaceID inject a face embedding captured from a single reference image directly into the denoising process, no training required~\cite{wang2024instantid,ye2023ipadapter}. The consent asymmetry this creates is severe---the subject never agreed to any of it---and it has triggered an arms race in \emph{protective perturbation}: imperceptible or lightly perceptible cloaks that, added to a photo before upload, are designed to break the customization pipeline. Anti-DreamBooth established the category~\cite{le2023antidreambooth}, and a fast-growing successor literature hardens it along every laboratory axis: meta-learned cross-user perturbations~\cite{liu2023metacloak}, targeted attacks that sharpen the failure mode~\cite{zheng2023targeted}, high-frequency designs that survive smoothing~\cite{onikubo2024highfrequency}, prompt-independent formulations~\cite{li2024promptindependent}, and protections aimed specifically at zero-shot identity adapters~\cite{song2024idprotector}. The narrative embedded in this literature is reassuring: with the right perturbation, your face is yours again.

This paper argues that the reassurance is an artifact of where the measurements are taken. Every published protection is evaluated on \emph{T0-images}: clean, full-resolution, uncompressed files that the adversary kindly provides to the defense. That world does not exist on the web. An identity that is ``public''---a social-media profile, a news photo, a conference page---has already been transformed before a scraper ever sees it: platforms re-encode uploads at quality levels set by storage and bandwidth budgets, browsers rasterize images under device pixel ratios and thumbnail pipelines, crawling agents screenshot pages and crop detected faces rather than download files, well-meaning ``enhancement'' super-resolves low-resolution re-uploads, and users themselves regenerate their photos through diffusion-based filters and editors before posting. Each transformation is individually harmless to recognition quality; jointly, they describe the actual data distribution that any real adversary can harvest. We call this pipeline the \emph{web acquisition funnel}, and we claim that it is not a nuisance variable for anti-customization protections but their primary adversary---an attack surface that requires no adversarial optimization whatsoever, costs milliseconds to seconds per image on commodity platforms, and operates \emph{before} the defense is ever consulted.

Two observations sharpen the claim. First, the funnel is not hypothetical: published measurements already show that a single JPEG re-encode at quality 85--95 neutralizes a broad class of protection perturbations~\cite{sandovalsegura2023jpeg}, that common image transformations systematically suppress unlearnable-example attacks~\cite{qin2023learning,sandovalsegura2023what}, and that robustness evaluations of protective perturbations under diffusion-aware adversaries remain unfavorable~\cite{peng2025contrastive}. What is missing is a systematic, tiered, budget-aware account of the entire funnel---from platform transcode to agent capture to restoration to regeneration---and of \emph{where along the funnel} each family of defenses actually collapses, measured under both tunable and zero-shot attack pipelines. Second, the evaluation practices of the field are drifting in the opposite direction: newest defenses arrive with evaluations on ever-cleaner galleries under ever-stronger laboratory attackers~\cite{fardin2026metacloakjpeg,chen2025fragile}, and the first cross-method evaluation studies, while valuable, still hold the acquisition side fixed~\cite{wang2025privacy}. A defense literature that only measures T0 retention is optimizing for an adversary that does not exist.

We build the missing instrument. \emph{ScrapeBench} reconstructs the funnel in software: seven tiers (T0 original; T1 platform transcode---JPEG/WebP quality sweeps and platform-style resize; T2 browser and thumbnail rendering at device pixel ratios; T3 agent-style page capture with face cropping; T4 restoration via super-resolution; T5 diffusion-based regeneration via img2img and DDIM inversion; T6 adaptive clean-source mixing and purification), each with an explicit attacker budget. On top of it we define the \emph{retention ratio} $\mathrm{RR}(T)=\Delta(T)/\Delta(T_0)$, where $\Delta(T)$ is the protection gap (clean minus protected identity similarity under a fixed attack) at tier $T$: RR measures the fraction of a defense's headline T0 protection that survives acquisition reality. The metric is deliberately paired with a cost ledger, because the funnel's defining property is an \emph{inversion of asymmetry}---the defender pays GPU-minutes per identity to forge a perturbation, while the attacker pays CPU-milliseconds per image to defeat it.

Running ScrapeBench over 40 public identities, four defenses spanning the protection design space (clean control, random noise, FSMG-style untargeted surrogate attacks~\cite{le2023antidreambooth}, and ASPL-lite alternation), and two attack families (DreamBooth-LoRA fine-tuning and zero-shot IP-Adapter-FaceID) yields a picture that we summarize in three findings, each with a headline number to be read from the collapse curves in Section~\ref{sec:results}: \textbf{(F1)} protection sheds most of its gap within the \emph{platform-controlled} tiers (T1--T2) that no defense can influence, before any adaptive attacker enters the picture; \textbf{(F2)} zero-shot identity adapters---which skip fine-tuning entirely---erase most of what survives the platform tiers, because the perturbations were never optimized against embedding-injection attacks; \textbf{(F3)} the cost asymmetry runs against the defender by orders of magnitude, converting ``protection'' from a guarantee into a tax that the attacker simply routes around. We accompany these findings with a constructive turn: a funnel-aware reporting standard (publish RR@T, not T0-only numbers), and an argument that the durable fix for face consent lies in provenance infrastructure---machine-readable consent manifests such as \texttt{face-consent.txt} served alongside images---rather than in ever-stronger perturbations, whose failure we show is structural.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{A funnel threat model.} We formalize web image acquisition as a seven-tier funnel with per-tier attacker budgets, making explicit the distribution shift that separates laboratory evaluations of protective perturbations from the data adversaries actually harvest.
\item \textbf{The retention-ratio metric and collapse-curve protocol.} RR$(T)$ reduces protection robustness under acquisition to a single interpretable curve per defense, and the paired cost ledger exposes the defender--attacker asymmetry that raw similarity numbers hide.
\item \textbf{ScrapeBench, an open harness.} We release a reproducible pipeline---platform transcoding, browser/agent rendering, restoration, diffusion regeneration, adaptive pooling---integrated with DreamBooth-LoRA and zero-shot IP-Adapter-FaceID attackers, ArcFace-based identity scoring, paired statistics, and automatic result publication.
\item \textbf{Empirical collapse findings.} Across 40 identities and four defenses we quantify F1--F3 above, including tier-resolved ablations over JPEG quality, device pixel ratio, and regeneration strength, establishing where---not just whether---protections fail.
\item \textbf{Implications and a constructive alternative.} We distill funnel-aware reporting guidelines for the protection literature and argue for consent-provenance infrastructure as the defense class that survives the funnel by construction.
\end{itemize}
