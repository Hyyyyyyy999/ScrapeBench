\section{Introduction}

The Web's old social contract was written for human readers. A person could
publish a portrait to be seen without thereby authorizing anyone to construct a
generator of their identity. On the agentic Web, that distinction is collapsing:
crawlers can collect a few images and invoke DreamBooth~\cite{ruiz2022dreambooth},
InstantID~\cite{wang2024instantid}, or IP-Adapter-FaceID~\cite{ye2023ipadapter}
without further interaction with the subject. Pixel perturbations such as
Anti-DreamBooth, GAP-Diff, and IDProtector attempt to restore agency by making
published images unsuitable for customization~\cite{le2023antidreambooth,zhu2025gapdiff,song2024idprotector}.

\textbf{What is brave and new.} We propose that anti-customization be treated
as an \emph{acquisition contract} between people, Web infrastructure, and AI
agents, not as a property of a single downloaded file. The concrete contribution
is a benchmark and reference defense in which ordinary Web interventions are
first-class, independently testable contract clauses. This changes the research
question from ``does noise fool one model?'' to ``after which legitimate Web
operation does a person's refusal cease to be enforceable?'' The distinction
matters for future browsers, CDNs, agents, and policy: it makes failures
attributable and points to where consent must be preserved.

A cumulative funnel---where each stage inherits the damage of all earlier
stages---cannot answer that question: it confounds the effect of a JPEG encoder
with that of a crop or a diffusion reconstruction. ScrapeBench instead defines
seven \emph{single interventions}. T0 is the released image; each of T1--T6
consumes that same T0 source independently. Clean and protected branches use
identical identities, operations, prompts, seeds, and scorers. This paired
design measures the protection gap attributable to one intervention while
separately recording whether the input or generated face remains usable.

Our preregistered feasibility pilot takes the first four valid identities from
a manifest fixed before any defense outcome was inspected, with four gallery
and two held-out reference images each. The preregistered expansion pool is
pre-audited (40 identities, 160 gallery images, 96.25\% input-face detection,
.787 mean gallery--reference ArcFace similarity) and runs the identical
protocol. The completed pilot compares clean uploads, GAP-Diff, MetaCloak,
GoodAC, and our ARCP-Web under InstantID and IP-Adapter-FaceID; released
checkpoints are used where provided, and MetaCloak and GoodAC are evaluated
from their released code with disclosed Web-scenario adaptations. A separate
DreamBooth protocol is a gated second-stage experiment.

ARCP-Web is designed for this contract. Its matched-budget evaluation uses a
pixel-space $8/255$ cap, coarse/detail perturbation grids, texture-aware
amplitude allocation, and a differentiable port of InstantID's ArcFace
recognizer inside a balanced objective spanning T0--T6. Half of its updates
cover all interventions uniformly; the rest target the intervention currently
retaining the most identity information. This is a falsifiable design, not a
guarantee. We claim an advantage at a layer only if its paired confidence
interval supports one.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item an acquisition-contract formulation for face consent on the agentic Web;
  \item a non-cumulative T0--T6 benchmark with matched clean controls, explicit
  validity gates, and conditional plus end-to-end metrics;
  \item a preregistered four-identity feasibility pilot comparing released
  robustness-oriented defenses and two zero-shot customization families, with a
  pre-audited 40-identity expansion path;
  \item ARCP-Web, whose optimization allocates effort to its weakest acquisition
  intervention rather than optimizing only the pristine upload; and
  \item a machine-readable \texttt{face-consent.txt} direction for carrying
  consent when no pixel-only defense can satisfy the contract.
\end{itemize}
