\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The acquisition-contract view connects an algorithmic privacy question to Web
architecture. A failure at T1 implicates image-serving infrastructure; a failure
at T3 implicates what an agent observes after browser rendering; a failure at
T5 or T6 shows that a pixel-only refusal cannot survive generative mediation.
Independent layers make those diagnoses possible, while a cascade would not.

The four-identity feasibility pilot is intentionally a fast falsification test;
the preregistered 40-identity expansion then resolves its provisional ranking.
Across the 11 rankable reader--layer cells at $n=40$, the targeted defense
IDProtector holds the largest paired gap in every cell, ARCP-Web follows in ten
and trails IDProtector significantly at the official default in nine, and the
two become statistically indistinguishable under a strict common $8/255$ budget
in ten of eleven cells---with ARCP-Web uploading 5.4 dB more imperceptible
images. GAP-Diff, the strongest pristine-file defense in earlier comparisons,
nearly vanishes under acquisition, confirming that pristine-file rank order is
not predictive of acquisition survival. The generative tiers T5--T6 remain the
weakest for every defense, and the pilot's wide $n=4$ intervals explain why
two seed replicas still disagree with the final ordering in a minority of
cells.
Real platform upload/download traces, additional identity distributions, and
adaptive attacks remain necessary. ARCP-Web's training proxies approximate T4--T6
rather than differentiating through their exact evaluation implementations, so
transfer is an empirical risk. Threats to validity also include the single
generation seed per scenario in the expansion, detector dependence of all
reported metrics, and port fidelity for the two released-code defenses. Most
importantly, robustness of a perturbation does not create a legal or social
duty for an agent to respect it; that is why the technical benchmark is paired
with a consent-manifest design direction.
