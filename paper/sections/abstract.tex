A rapidly growing family of ``protective perturbation'' tools promises to make personal photos unlearnable by unauthorized text-to-image customization pipelines, and deployment of such tools has begun. Their guarantees, however, are measured in a laboratory world that no longer exists: clean full-resolution images, lossless pipelines, and tunable DreamBooth-style attackers. In practice, the images an adversary trains on are \emph{scraped}, and every scraped image has already passed through a lossy acquisition funnel---platform transcoding, browser and thumbnail rendering, agent-side capture, super-resolution ``restoration,'' and diffusion-based regeneration. We present \emph{ScrapeBench}, the first evaluation harness that treats this acquisition funnel as a first-class attack surface against anti-customization protections. ScrapeBench formalizes a seven-level funnel (T0--T6) with explicit per-image attacker budgets, and introduces the \emph{retention ratio} RR$(T)$, which measures how much of a defense's clean-domain protection survives at funnel tier $T$. Across 40 identities from a public celebrity face corpus, four representative defenses, and two attack families (fine-tuning-based DreamBooth-LoRA and zero-shot IP-Adapter-FaceID), we find that protection erodes monotonically with funnel depth: defenses that are considered strong at T0 retain only a small fraction of their protection gap by mid-funnel, and zero-shot adapters---which require no training at all---close most of the remaining gap. We further quantify a stark cost asymmetry: perturbing an identity costs hundreds of GPU-seconds, while the funnel transformations that defeat it cost milliseconds on commodity platforms. Our results argue that protection claims must be reported funnel-aware, and that the durable fix lies not in stronger perturbations but in consent and provenance infrastructure. We release the ScrapeBench harness, configurations, and all measured curves.
